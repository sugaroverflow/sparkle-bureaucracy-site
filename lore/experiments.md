# Prototype and Experiments Log

This log turns the Sparkle Bureaucracy experiment lab into assessable experiments. Each experiment should be specific enough to test, discuss with faculty, and schedule.

The prototype is Sparkle Bureaucracy as an experiment lab. The current recommendation is to treat the agent-assisted prototype diary and faculty feedback loop as lab infrastructure, while using faculty feedback to choose the strongest first public/civic-facing experiment.

See [experiment-research-sprint.md](./experiment-research-sprint.md) for the consolidated research memo comparing the main candidate experiments.

---

## Experiment Selection Criteria

Use these questions to compare candidate experiments:

- What civic or political community would use or test it?
- What bureaucratic ritual does it redesign?
- What is the smallest credible version?
- What evidence would show that it worked?
- What could fail methodologically?
- What would faculty be able to critique?
- What would be reusable by someone else?

## Experiment 1: Agent-Assisted Prototype Diary

Status: Planned

Date opened: 2026-06-19

### Hypothesis

An agent-assisted diary can make the prototype work more legible by turning scattered notes, links, Matrix posts, and feedback into structured weekly diary drafts, experiment cards, prior-art candidates, and collaborator suggestions.

### Method

Start with a human-reviewed markdown workflow:

1. Export or copy notes from Matrix, GitHub, voice notes, or faculty conversations.
2. Classify each note by type: link, journal, question, project, idea, field note, engagement evidence, prior art, experiment.
3. Draft weekly diary entries and experiment updates.
4. Keep source links attached.
5. Require human approval before publishing to the repo.

## Experiment 2: Faculty Feedback Loop

Status: Planned

Date opened: 2026-06-19

### Hypothesis

Faculty will give more actionable feedback when asked specific decision-shaped questions than when asked to review the whole project narrative.

### Method

Run two feedback surfaces in parallel:

1. A broad commentable document or repo page.
2. A structured feedback prompt with one decision, options, current belief, what would change my mind, and requested critique.

Track which format produces more concrete changes to the prototype.

Status: Planned

Date opened: 2026-06-19

## Candidate Prototype Cards

### A. Synthetic Voice in Participatory Channels

Working question: How should representatives and civic organisations handle constituent feedback when synthetic voice is cheap to generate?

Potential community: civic engagement teams, representatives, campaigners, consultation designers, civic tech practitioners.

Bureaucratic ritual: public consultation, constituent inboxes, feedback forms, petitions, comment periods.

Possible intervention: a ritual/interface that classifies, verifies, aggregates, or makes uncertainty visible without silencing authentic participation.

Evidence to collect: practitioner interviews, prototype walkthroughs, adversarial test cases, sample feedback flows.

Key risk: hardening against synthetic feedback could also make access harder for real constituents.

### B. Hybrid Liquid Democracy Election

Working question: What happens when a real community election uses both paper and digital voting, with visible delegation and revocation?

Potential community: Newspeak House or another bounded community with low-stakes but real decisions.

Bureaucratic ritual: election, delegation, ballot handling, tallying, legitimacy ceremony.

Possible intervention: a side-by-side election ritual that makes trust in paper, screen, delegation, and counting visible.

Evidence to collect: turnout, participant interviews, trust ratings, objections, failure notes, comparison of paper and digital experience.

Key risk: a voting experiment creates legitimacy expectations even when it is framed as exploratory.

### C. Digital Identity / Verification Ritual

Working question: What does it mean to prove who you are when identity verification, synthetic media, and AI agents collide?

Potential community: civic tech builders, event organisers, digital identity practitioners, public-service designers.

Bureaucratic ritual: identity proofing, credential checking, eligibility verification, trust documents.

Possible intervention: a transparent verification ceremony or prototype that makes the trade-offs in identity systems inspectable.

Evidence to collect: walkthrough feedback, failure cases, accessibility concerns, trust and discomfort reactions.

Key risk: identity systems can easily become exclusionary, especially for people already failed by documentation systems.

### D. DMV / Queue and Classification Ritual

Working question: Can a frustrating service encounter be made more legible and humane without changing the underlying policy?

Potential community: public-service designers, local government teams, people who have experienced high-friction government services.

Bureaucratic ritual: waiting, classification, form intake, eligibility checks, ticket numbers, handoffs.

Possible intervention: a queue or classification simulation that makes the process understandable, participatory, and emotionally less hostile.

Evidence to collect: participant walkthroughs, before/after comprehension, perceived fairness, emotional response, staff/practitioner critique.

Key risk: making a bad process feel better may obscure the need to change the underlying policy.
