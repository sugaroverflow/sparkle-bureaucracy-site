# Movements and Tactics We're Inspired By

Status: companion research framework and founder-nominated seed cases
Research date: 30 July 2026

The subsequent deep research run is in [`movements-deep-research.md`](./movements-deep-research.md). It contains the calibrated movement rubric, a sourced 25-case longlist, twelve full evidence profiles, an eight-case website recommendation, private reserves, and the resulting design principles. This file retains the two cases that prompted the run and the reasoning for treating movements separately from authored projects.

This is a companion to the project longlist, not a fifth project classification. Movements often have diffuse authorship, changing names, contested boundaries and no single deliverable. Treating them as products would erase the collective action that makes them powerful.

The website-facing title can be **Movements we're inspired by**. Internally, research both the movement and the specific tactic: glitter, costume, humour, song, ritual, occupation, mutual aid, counter-documentation or another affective device.

## Why this belongs in Sparkle Bureaucracy

Movements can reveal a different form of Sparkle:

- Affect is a method of civic action, not a decorative communications layer.
- Humour, beauty, camp, absurdity, grief or rage can change who feels able to enter a confrontation.
- A memorable material or image can interrupt the state's preferred account of an event.
- The tactic can make coercive institutions visible while refusing their emotional script.
- Replication can leave behind a distributed capability even when there is no organisation, platform or formal publication.

These cases should not be scored against the complete project rubric. Research them through:

```text
Movement and tactic
Date and place
Institutional power confronted
Material or affective device
Feeling or perception designed into the action
How the tactic changes participation, visibility or narrative
How it circulates or becomes replicable
Who is taking the risk
Power, safety, appropriation and media-framing cautions
What Sparkle Bureaucracy borrows
Sources and attribution limits
```

## Starter cases

### 1. Mexico's Glitter Revolution / #NoMeCuidanMeViolan

- **Date and place:** Mexico City and other Mexican cities, August 2019 onward.
- **Movement/tactic:** Feminist protests against violence toward women, institutional impunity and alleged police sexual violence used pink glitter as a collective symbol and direct intervention. Protesters threw glitter at Mexico City's police chief; it also became part of signs, bodies and the wider visual language of the demonstrations.
- **Institutional power confronted:** Police violence, the handling of complaints and investigations, official credibility, and the wider failure of the state to prevent or respond to gender-based violence and femicide.
- **Designed affect:** Glitter holds celebration, femininity and apparent frivolity while being invasive, adhesive and extremely difficult to contain. Against uniforms, offices and police procedure, it turns rage into a visible contamination of official authority. Its sparkle does not soften the demand; it makes the refusal harder to control or forget.
- **How it changes participation and narrative:** The material is inexpensive, portable and recognisable. It creates an image that can travel beyond a march while rejecting the expectation that political anger must look solemn or respectable to count.
- **Durable capability:** A replicable protest language rather than a formal organisation or tool.
- **What SB borrows:** Choose a material whose cultural meaning conflicts with the institution's emotional script; let the affective device mark power rather than merely decorate participants; create symbols that can be copied without central permission.
- **Cautions:** Do not reduce demands about violence and impunity to an attractive visual identity. Credit the protesters and political conditions, not the publication that later explained the tactic. Glitter also carries environmental and safety concerns that should not be casually reproduced.
- **Source:** Lauren Cocking, [“Mexico's Glitter Revolution Is Taking Aim at Violence Against Women”](https://www.teenvogue.com/story/mexico-protest-glitter-explainer), *Teen Vogue*, 28 August 2019.

### 2. Operation Inflation and Portland's inflatable anti-ICE protesters

- **Date and place:** Portland, Oregon; the inflatable-costume tactic expanded during anti-ICE demonstrations in 2025 and continued as a recognisable protest form into 2026.
- **Movement/tactic:** After an inflatable frog appeared outside Portland's ICE facility, protesters adopted frogs and other oversized animal costumes. The distributed effort became associated with **Operation Inflation**, which helped make costumes available to more participants.
- **Institutional power confronted:** Immigration detention and enforcement, armed federal presence, policing of protest, and official narratives portraying opposition as inherently threatening or disorderly.
- **Designed affect:** Absurdity, humour, joy and ridicule are placed directly against uniforms, weapons and institutional seriousness. The soft, ungainly bodies make it difficult to sustain a purely menacing visual account of the crowd. The costumes do not deny danger; they refuse to let the state determine the encounter's emotional register.
- **How it changes participation and narrative:** A costume supplies an immediately legible role, a shared visual identity and a tactic that others can repeat. Its media visibility helped the image travel beyond one protest and turn a lone frog into a distributed repertoire.
- **Durable capability:** A recognisable, low-instruction protest format and a growing shared supply of costumes rather than a conventional project artifact.
- **What SB borrows:** Use absurdity to expose the theatricality already present in state power; create roles people can inhabit quickly; design a visual contradiction that survives hostile framing; let a symbol become collective rather than keeping it as a branded mascot.
- **Cautions:** Spectacle must not eclipse people directly harmed by immigration enforcement. Costumes do not remove physical or legal risk, and reporting about the tactic should not be mistaken for evidence about the movement's full demands or organisation.
- **Sources:** [Oregon Public Broadcasting reporting on the adoption of Operation Inflation costumes](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMilAFBVV95cUxQTXNOaXREZzVxZFFiUUFQVkRqR2k5UkttNE1NTzZqUktOOFFDUmxSaDlkN0xLNm5kdVZGR19XcGt0cHhQWkxvVkU3aENpMElTZW9xdHlzbjdLTEtuSnVRTi02ZERGQzRqS3pQcmNOSDhvZFQtYzR1dUExZzQ4dDJzQ2trTFJUUzM1UktncEtYS2hOcXlL?oc=5), 30 March 2026; additional reporting should be source-audited before publication.

## Editorial guardrails

- Attribute collective tactics carefully; do not invent a singular designer.
- Separate the movement's political demands from the tactic through which it became visually legible.
- Ask who is exposed to arrest, retaliation, surveillance or misrepresentation.
- Do not treat virality as civic impact.
- Do not praise “playfulness” when it trivialises harm or recentres spectators over affected communities.
- Record what the tactic changes: participation, morale, narrative, visibility, material support or institutional response.
- Prefer movements whose affect reveals or contests power, rather than making coercion more pleasant.

## Discovery directions used in the deep run

The private research pool is broad, while the proposed public section remains small and globally varied. The search included:

- feminist and queer protest aesthetics;
- carnivalesque and absurdist resistance;
- protest puppetry, inflatables, projection and light;
- songs, chants, mourning rituals and collective silence;
- counter-mapping, witnessing and citizen documentation;
- disability-led access tactics and mutual-aid rituals;
- movements that appropriate forms, uniforms, receipts, permits or official language;
- cases where a compelling aesthetic was co-opted or obscured the underlying demand.

Eight well-contextualised cases are recommended for the first website set. The 25-case research list remains private infrastructure for future writing and programming.
