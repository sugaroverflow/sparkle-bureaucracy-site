# Movements and Affective Tactics: Deep Research Run

Status: evidence-backed longlist and editorial recommendation
Research date: 30 July 2026
Scope: collective tactics that make institutional power felt, interrupt its emotional script, and give people a reusable way to act

## Outcome

The two founder-nominated anchors—Mexico's Glitter Revolution and Portland's inflatable anti-ICE protests—show that this is not simply a search for “creative protest.” The useful research unit has two layers:

1. **The movement:** the people, demand, political conditions and institutional power being confronted.
2. **The tactic:** the material, role, ritual, choreography or image through which that confrontation becomes felt and repeatable.

Treating only the second layer as the work would turn political struggle into an aesthetic reference board. Treating only the first would miss the designed affect that makes these cases relevant to Sparkle Bureaucracy.

### Provisional definition

> **Movement Sparkle is an affective civic tactic that makes institutional power possible to feel, gives people an inhabitable way to confront it, and leaves behind a repertoire others can reuse—without disguising the stakes or transferring attention away from those taking the risk.**

This is a companion to the main Sparkle definition, not a replacement for it. A movement tactic does not need AI, a formal organisation, a publication, or reception research to be instructive. Its durable artifact may be a symbol, score, role, gesture, chant, garment or repeated procedure.

## Calibrated research rubric

The original project rubric still contributes two questions: what bureaucracy or consequential process is touched, and what affect is designed? Movement cases need five further tests.

1. **Institutional target:** Which law, office, police power, administrative failure, negotiation or public narrative is being confronted?
2. **Affective mechanism:** What does the material or action do—not merely signify—to alter fear, grief, ridicule, dignity, solidarity or attention?
3. **Inhabitable role:** Does the tactic give participants something comprehensible to wear, do, repeat, carry, sing or make?
4. **Strategic change:** Is there evidence that it altered participation, public narrative, movement morale, institutional response or material capacity?
5. **Distributed durability:** Can it travel without a central author, and what knowledge or infrastructure survives the moment?
6. **Power legibility:** Does it expose coercion and open room for agency, or merely make confrontation attractive to spectators?
7. **Risk and attribution:** Who faces arrest, violence, surveillance, appropriation or misrepresentation, and whose authorship is being simplified?

### Four separate evidence claims

Do not collapse these into one “impact” score:

- **Designed affect:** readable in the form and supported by maker or critical accounts.
- **Experienced response:** reported by participants or observers; useful but not required to recognise affective design.
- **Strategic effect:** a change in participation, framing, morale, replication or institutional behaviour.
- **Political outcome:** a law, decision, negotiation or allocation changed. Attribution will almost always be shared and contested.

Virality is evidence of circulation, not evidence of civic impact. An attractive image without an institutional target or participatory role belongs in a visual reference folder, not this list.

## Six discovery lanes

1. **Material transformation:** glitter, cloth, umbrellas, brooms and everyday objects whose ordinary meaning is turned against authority.
2. **Counter-uniforms and roles:** costumes or dress codes that let people inhabit a collective civic character.
3. **Choreography and collective voice:** repeated movement, song, silence, call-and-response and sound.
4. **Witnessing, grief and counter-archives:** names, bodies, memorial forms and rituals that refuse administrative disappearance.
5. **Counter-procedure:** citizen-made assemblies, vigils, marches and negotiations that answer a failed or exclusionary official process.
6. **Spectacle and dilemma:** humour, absurdity or technology that makes repression reputationally costly and inaction permissive.

## Twelve deeper evidence profiles

### 1. Glitter Revolution / `#NoMeCuidanMeViolan`

- **Movement and demand:** Feminist protests followed allegations of sexual violence by police and anger at institutional impunity. The demand was justice and safety, not better communications about policing.
- **Tactic:** Pink glitter moved from an individual throw at Mexico City's police chief into faces, bodies, signs, hashtags and demonstrations. Later research describes the material and online circulation as one embodied-digital action rather than separate “real” and “social” protests.
- **Designed affect:** Glitter is culturally coded as decorative, feminine and unserious, yet materially it spreads, sticks and resists cleanup. The tactic makes rage luminous and contaminates the official's controlled appearance. It neither asks power to smile nor makes the police process gentler.
- **Experienced / observed response:** News framing frequently centred damaged monuments or disorder rather than gender violence. That is important counter-evidence: Sparkle can interrupt authority's story while still being re-contained by another institutional frame.
- **Civic mechanism:** A cheap material became a decentralised identifying language and a media image capable of carrying the underlying accusation.
- **Durable capability:** A reusable symbolic grammar—glitter as both armour and trace—rather than a platform or organisation.
- **Risk:** The visual may be stripped from the allegations, organisers and physical risk that give it meaning. Environmental and ocular-safety concerns also make literal copying inappropriate.
- **What SB borrows:** Choose a material that behaves politically; let it mark power, not just decorate the campaign; design for hostile framing as well as friendly circulation.
- **Confidence:** High on designed affect and circulation; medium on attributable institutional effect.
- **Evidence:** [founder-nominated explainer](https://www.teenvogue.com/story/mexico-protest-glitter-explainer); [material/digital analysis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01436597.2025.2456839); [media-framing study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14680777.2024.2418378).

### 2. Operation Inflation / Portland's inflatable anti-ICE protesters

- **Movement and demand:** Protesters confronted immigration detention and enforcement at Portland's ICE facility, amid federal claims that the city and demonstrations were violent or out of control.
- **Tactic:** After an inflatable frog became recognisable, organisers associated with Operation Inflation distributed more inflatable animal costumes. OPB reports that a practical aim was to make contemporary footage unmistakable, frustrating the reuse of old protest imagery.
- **Designed affect:** The costumes create soft, ridiculous and physically awkward bodies opposite armour, chemical agents and federal seriousness. Humour is not an escape from risk: AP documented a frog-costumed protester being pepper-sprayed through the suit's air vent.
- **Experienced / observed response:** Participants described the tactic as lighthearted and less frightening; reporters and organisers also observed its usefulness as a counter-image to official narratives of a threatening crowd.
- **Civic mechanism:** A costume supplies a role, lowers the instruction burden, helps date and authenticate imagery, and turns an individual visual gag into shared protest infrastructure.
- **Durable capability:** A costume library, a recognisable repertoire and practical knowledge about performing it.
- **Risk:** Spectacle can recentre comfortable spectators, obscure detained people, or invite the mistaken claim that humour makes the encounter safe.
- **What SB borrows:** Build a role people can enter quickly; make the interface resistant to miscaptioning and archival reuse; use absurdity to expose the theatre already operating in institutional force.
- **Confidence:** High on the tactic's organisation, intent and immediate reception; low on long-term political effect because the campaign is recent.
- **Evidence:** [OPB interview](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/03/30/think-out-loud-operation-inflation-animal-costumes/); [AP reporting](https://apnews.com/article/7017b62b45eab1cac82a169a763af359); [Portland Frog Brigade](https://www.portlandfrogbrigade.com/about).

### 3. LASTESIS, *Un violador en tu camino*

- **Movement and demand:** The Chilean feminist collective created the performance in the context of sexual violence and the role of police, courts and the state in reproducing impunity.
- **Tactic:** A short sequence of words, blindfolds, squats, pointing and steps functions as a score. Groups across more than 50 countries translated and adapted it while retaining the accusation that the state and its institutions are implicated.
- **Designed affect:** Synchrony turns isolated testimony and imposed shame into collective certainty. The choreography is simple enough to learn, strong enough to read from a distance, and direct enough to return the gaze to authorities.
- **Experienced / observed response:** A LASTESIS member described the action as allowing participants to “burn fear”; accounts also connect its repetition with stories of sexual violence being disclosed. At the same time, the collective faced police complaints and legal harassment.
- **Civic mechanism:** The score distributes authorship without dissolving the demand. Translation creates local ownership while the repeated structure makes international solidarity legible.
- **Durable capability:** A portable protocol for collective accusation.
- **Risk:** Re-performance can become a decontextualised cultural event, and public participation can expose survivors or invite retaliation.
- **What SB borrows:** Write a score, not just a message; let repetition create safety and force; design variation points so local groups can translate without losing the institutional target.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Evidence:** [performance research](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13528165.2024.2408118); [UNAM interview](https://coordinaciongenero.unam.mx/2021/06/performance-un-violador-en-tu-camino-nos-permite-quemar-el-miedo-lastesis/); [Artists at Risk Connection](https://artistsatriskconnection.org/artist-voice/lastesis/).

### 4. Otpor!'s Milošević barrel

- **Movement and demand:** Serbia's Otpor! movement sought to end Slobodan Milošević's authoritarian rule.
- **Tactic:** Activists placed a barrel painted with Milošević's face in a shopping district and invited passers-by to pay for a strike. When police arrived after the organisers had withdrawn, they arrested the barrel.
- **Designed affect:** The laughter is structurally produced by the opponent's options. Ignoring the barrel permits open ridicule; repressing it makes the state look frightened of an object. This is a **dilemma action**, not humour added after strategy.
- **Experienced / observed response:** Images of police taking the barrel away circulated as a joke at the regime's expense; later movement and nonviolent-action literature repeatedly preserves it as a teachable tactic.
- **Civic mechanism:** It let low-risk passers-by make a small public gesture and invited the authorities to demonstrate the disproportion the movement alleged.
- **Durable capability:** A strategic pattern—design the opponent's response options—not merely the barrel itself.
- **Risk:** “Dilemma” is not magic; the same action under a more violent or less media-sensitive regime can expose participants without reputational cost to authorities.
- **What SB borrows:** Prototype the institution's possible responses; make coercion self-describing; let participation begin with a small but meaningful act.
- **Confidence:** High on the action and strategic reading; political outcome belongs to the broader movement.
- **Evidence:** [Museum of Protest account](https://museumofprotest.org/methods/humorous-skits-and-pranks/); [open-access research on dilemma actions](https://d119vjm4apzmdm.cloudfront.net/open-access/pdfs/9781501756078.pdf); [Guardian profile of Otpor! strategist Srđa Popović](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2015/mar/08/srdja-popovic-revolution-serbian-activist-protest).

### 5. Mothers of Plaza de Mayo

- **Movement and demand:** Mothers sought information and accountability for children forcibly disappeared by Argentina's military dictatorship.
- **Tactic:** They gathered at the square facing the presidential palace. Police told them to keep moving under emergency rules, so they walked around the pyramid in pairs. White headscarves—first children's nappies—made kinship, care and the absent people visible.
- **Designed affect:** Maternal care is not used to soften the accusation. A domestic object becomes a counter-uniform; repetitive circling turns forced movement into a weekly civic procedure the state did not provide.
- **Experienced / observed response:** The scarves and Thursday marches remain recognisable across generations. Oral histories describe names embroidered into the cloth and the scarf becoming inseparable from public identity and memory.
- **Civic mechanism:** Recurrence creates an alternative public record. The ritual holds space, teaches newcomers, preserves names, and prevents disappearance from becoming administrative closure.
- **Durable capability:** A schedule, place, garment, archive and intergenerational role.
- **Risk:** The language of motherhood can narrow whose grief is publicly legible; the organisations also have complex political histories that a short card cannot flatten.
- **What SB borrows:** When the institution withholds status, build a counter-procedure that returns on a known cadence; allow a humble object to carry names and continuity.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Evidence:** [official history](https://madresplazademayolf.org.ar/madres/historia/); [CIPDH–UNESCO](https://www.cipdh.gob.ar/memorias-situadas/en/lugar-de-memoria/ronda-de-las-madres-de-plaza-de-mayo/); [AP interviews](https://apnews.com/article/45ce0f55238e9a60548825f3deb3fb32).

### 6. ACT UP: SILENCE=DEATH, die-ins and the FDA action

- **Movement and demand:** ACT UP confronted public-health indifference, inaccessible drug trials, slow FDA approval, insurance exclusions and the political abandonment of people with AIDS.
- **Tactic:** The movement combined a compressed visual language with teach-ins, affinity-group planning, die-ins and technically informed direct action. At the FDA in 1988, administrative procedure itself was a target, not just the backdrop.
- **Designed affect:** The black field, pink triangle and imperative transform silence from absence into complicity. Die-ins make delayed decisions visible as bodies while disciplined preparation resists the dismissal of anger as ignorance.
- **Experienced / observed response:** The actions generated mass media attention and helped force engagement with activists' detailed proposals. The affect was intentionally mixed: grief, anger, urgency, erotic life and collective competence.
- **Civic mechanism:** ACT UP joined emotional force to counter-expertise. Participants learned the bureaucracy well enough to challenge its categories, timelines and rules.
- **Durable capability:** Visual assets, meeting practice, treatment literacy, direct-action knowledge and a large archive.
- **Risk:** A famous poster can erase the less photogenic care, research and internal political work that made the intervention effective.
- **What SB borrows:** Pair an unforgettable emotional interface with procedural literacy; make the rule and the human consequence visible at the same time; design routes from attention into organised competence.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Evidence:** [ACT UP New York archive](https://actupny.org/); [history of die-ins and FDA action](https://www.history.com/articles/aids-activism-protests-act-up-die-ins); [ACT UP Oral History Project](https://actuporalhistory.org/).

### 7. Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace

- **Movement and demand:** Christian and Muslim women organised across social divisions to demand an end to Liberia's civil war and entry into a peace process from which ordinary women were largely excluded.
- **Tactic:** Thousands wore white, prayed and sat together near Monrovia's fish market. Women later travelled to the peace talks in Ghana and blocked doors when negotiations stalled.
- **Designed affect:** White creates a common counter-uniform and a claim to peace without requiring doctrinal sameness. Repeated sitting makes endurance visible; linking arms converts moral witness into a physical constraint on a failed process.
- **Experienced / observed response:** Research records sorrow and frustration as organising conditions, the unprecedented Christian–Muslim coalition, and the movement's later voter-registration work.
- **Civic mechanism:** The action moved from public witness to access, negotiation pressure and post-war democratic participation.
- **Durable capability:** Cross-religious organising relationships, WIPNET networks and a tactic later adapted elsewhere.
- **Risk:** Popular retellings often reduce a complex campaign to a “sex strike” or attribute a peace agreement to one tactic, erasing organisers, negotiations and other political forces.
- **What SB borrows:** A counter-uniform can hold difference rather than erase it; the strongest affective action has an escalation path from symbol to leverage and then to civic infrastructure.
- **Confidence:** High on organising and tactics; medium on singular causal claims about the peace outcome.
- **Evidence:** [peer-reviewed account](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/7/323); [USIP action guide](https://www.usip.org/sites/default/files/2019-04/snap-synergizing-nonviolent-action-and-peacebuilding-action-guide.pdf); [UW–Madison case study](https://thenonviolenceproject.wisc.edu/2024/02/04/how-women-ended-liberias-civil-war-women-of-liberia-mass-action-for-peace/).

### 8. Hīkoi mō te Tiriti and the parliamentary haka

- **Movement and demand:** A nationwide hīkoi opposed legislation that sought to redefine the principles of Te Tiriti o Waitangi. Tens of thousands arrived at Parliament; the bill was later defeated at second reading.
- **Tactic:** Hīkoi, waiata, signs and wero carried the challenge across the country. During the bill's first reading, Hana-Rāwhiti Maipi-Clarke tore a copy and led a haka joined by MPs and people in the public gallery.
- **Designed affect:** The action inserts a living Indigenous protocol into the legislature's procedural script. The document is not only argued against; it is physically refused while collective voice crosses the chamber–gallery boundary.
- **Experienced / observed response:** The parliamentary video circulated globally and the hīkoi drew a very large, largely peaceful crowd. Three MPs later received record suspensions, making institutional control of legitimate expression part of the case.
- **Civic mechanism:** Embodied action and mass mobilisation remained tied to a precise legislative object, submission process and vote.
- **Durable capability:** Intergenerational mobilisation, political education and a repertoire grounded in Māori practice—not a generic performance asset.
- **Risk:** Haka must not be extracted as an energising “format” by organisations without standing or cultural authority. The parliamentary discipline and continuing constitutional struggle are part of the story.
- **What SB borrows:** Bring living protocol into direct contact with official procedure; make the contested document tangible; never confuse learning from culturally grounded action with permission to copy it.
- **Confidence:** High on action, participation and legislative result; political causation is collective.
- **Evidence:** [RNZ hīkoi record](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=32tRNbmsoo4); [official bill record](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/bill/government/2024/0094/latest/LMS1003448.html); [AP account of the parliamentary action](https://apnews.com/article/b85059be2620fc08b21ae06f62ab5079); [AP account of the bill's defeat](https://apnews.com/article/41454ca84aef1a52feb3f2f0d03aa856).

### 9. Green Wave / pañuelazo

- **Movement and demand:** Argentina's National Campaign for the Right to Legal, Safe and Free Abortion brought a long organising history into legislative debate and helped inspire allied movements across Latin America.
- **Tactic:** Green handkerchiefs made support portable and visible. They could be worn, tied to bags, held above crowds and reproduced cheaply without a central supplier.
- **Designed affect:** Green was selected as a hopeful colour not already monopolised by a political party. The cloth recalls the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo while creating a distinct generational and political identity.
- **Civic mechanism:** The symbol connects street pressure, education, coalition growth and a concrete legislative demand rather than substituting for them.
- **Durable capability:** A movement identity that travelled across borders and remained legible beyond a single vote.
- **Risk:** Symbol histories are often retrospectively simplified; national differences in law, risk and movement leadership disappear if every green-handkerchief action is treated as one campaign.
- **What SB borrows:** A small wearable artifact can make a latent constituency countable to itself and visible to an institution; colour works when attached to organising infrastructure and a decision point.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Evidence:** [Amnesty account](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/impact/2019/08/the-green-wave/); [CONICET research](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/handle/11336/168313); [ELA campaign report](https://ela.org.ar/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/2019-De-la-clandestinidad-al-Congreso-INGLES.pdf).

### 10. AIDS Memorial Quilt

- **Movement and demand:** The NAMES Project emerged amid governmental neglect, stigma and an epidemic whose deaths were often depersonalised or hidden.
- **Tactic:** Each panel is approximately three by six feet—the size of a grave—and is made from clothing, photographs, names, jokes and other personal materials. Thousands of panels assemble into a memorial whose physical scale cannot be understood as a statistic alone.
- **Designed affect:** Domestic fabric and intimate detail offer care without suppressing accusation. The contrast between one life and overwhelming accumulation produces grief, tenderness and political scale at once.
- **Experienced / observed response:** Contemporary and later accounts describe visitors becoming silent or overwhelmed; the Quilt also became an education and prevention resource.
- **Civic mechanism:** Making a panel gives bereaved people a task, a contribution and a public record. Display turns private loss into collective evidence and brings it physically to the seat of government.
- **Durable capability:** Nearly 50,000 panels, panel-maker records, stewardship, searchable digital access and a distributed making protocol.
- **Risk:** Memorialisation can be detached from the movement's demands, and institutional stewardship can freeze a living political object into heritage.
- **What SB borrows:** Give scale a human unit; allow distributed contributions to remain individually legible inside a vast whole; treat the archive as part of the civic interface.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Evidence:** [National AIDS Memorial](https://www.aidsmemorial.org/quilt); [Library of Congress panel-maker records](https://www.loc.gov/collections/aids-memorial-quilt-records/about-this-collection/); [Smithsonian history](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/aids-quilt-memorial).

### 11. Myanmar's pots-and-pans resistance

- **Movement and demand:** After the February 2021 military coup, people protested despite curfews, surveillance and rapidly escalating violence.
- **Tactic:** Residents struck pots and pans from homes in a nightly ritual adapted from a practice associated with driving away evil.
- **Designed affect:** A domestic object becomes a public instrument. Sound lets participants recognise one another without first assembling in one exposed crowd; fear is answered by neighbourhood-scale synchrony.
- **Civic mechanism:** The tactic creates an accessible first act of refusal and a rough acoustic map of support under constrained conditions.
- **Durable capability:** A time, rhythm and household repertoire requiring almost no specialist material.
- **Risk:** “Low threshold” does not mean safe: sound reveals location, state violence escalated, and international fascination with the ritual can eclipse the movement's human cost.
- **What SB borrows:** Design participation for the actual risk environment; existing household rituals may provide more accessible civic infrastructure than a new digital channel.
- **Confidence:** High on occurrence and meaning; medium on strategic contribution.
- **Evidence:** [Washington Post reporting](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2021/02/04/pots-pans-protests-myanmar-coup/); [UN human-rights report](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/HRBodies/HRC/RegularSessions/Session48/Documents/A_HRC_48_CRP2_EN.pdf).

### 12. Holograms for Freedom — useful near-miss

- **Movement and demand:** No Somos Delito opposed Spain's Citizen Security Law, which introduced sanctions affecting public protest.
- **Tactic:** People contributed images and messages to a projected “hologram” march outside Parliament, presented as a protest of representations in a place where physical demonstrators risked penalties.
- **Designed affect:** The form makes a sharp conceptual joke: only an absent, simulated citizen can safely appear. The technology turns legal chilling effect into a visible ghost public.
- **Civic mechanism:** It opened remote contribution and created a highly transmissible explanation of the law's perceived absurdity.
- **Why it is a near-miss:** The campaign was produced with a major advertising agency and is most often documented as an innovation or “first.” Public evidence is much stronger for spectacle and media circulation than for participant agency, durable organising capability or influence on the law.
- **What SB borrows:** Make a rule's consequence experiential and use emerging technology when it sharpens that argument.
- **What SB should not borrow:** Novelty as a proxy for redistribution of voice; a representation of participation without clarity about who controls the edit, projection and institutional handoff.
- **Confidence:** High on execution and affective concept; low-to-medium on civic effect.
- **Evidence:** [MIT Docubase](https://docubase.mit.edu/project/holograms-for-freedom/); [No Somos Delito](https://www.nosomosdelito.net/).

The strongest first set is not simply the eight most famous. Together these cases explain eight different ingredients:

### Why the two anchors both stay

They are not redundant “playful protest” cases.

- **Glitter** is a material intervention: it contaminates the official image and turns imposed unseriousness into force.
- **Inflatables** are a participatory role and evidentiary device: they let people enter an ensemble and help prevent old footage being passed off as new.

One marks authority; the other reorganises the crowd and the record.

## What this run changes about Sparkle

### 1. Sparkle can be oppositional

It need not make an encounter pleasant. Rage, grief, accusation, ridicule, uncanniness and disciplined silence can be designed affect when they help people perceive and contest power.

### 2. The relevant artifact may be a repertoire

Durability is not limited to software, a toolkit or an institution. A weekly circuit, a scarf, a dance score, a costume role or a call-and-response protocol can be reusable civic capability.

### 3. “Designer” may be the wrong noun

Some tactics have named artists or organisers; others emerge through collective copying and alteration. Research should ask how the form stabilised, who carried it and whose account is authoritative—not force every case into a singular creator credit.

### 4. Affect can change the evidence environment

Operation Inflation makes footage harder to misdate. The Quilt creates records where official counting erased lives. The Mothers keep names in a public weekly register. Affect is not only how something feels; sometimes it changes what can be seen, remembered or credibly denied.

### 5. The strongest tactics contain a route from feeling to action

The pañuelo can be worn; the LASTESIS score can be learned; the People's Mic requires listeners to speak; the Quilt panel can be made. Sparkle becomes civic when the audience has a next verb.

### 6. Spectacle is a design risk, not an automatic virtue

A polished image can win attention while leaving participation, contestability and institutional leverage unchanged. Holograms for Freedom belongs in the research precisely because its conceptual clarity exceeds the available evidence of durable civic capacity.

### 7. Cultural specificity limits borrowing

Sparkle Bureaucracy can borrow principles from haka, pañuelos or the Mothers' scarves; it cannot treat culturally and politically grounded forms as free-floating formats. “What can we learn?” and “what may we reproduce?” are different questions.

## Design principles for Sparkle Bureaucracy's own work

1. **Give people a next verb:** wear, stamp, carry, refuse, annotate, repeat, appeal, witness or make.
2. **Design the opponent's response set:** what happens if the institution ignores, accepts, delays or represses the intervention?
3. **Make the institutional target nameable:** the affecting form should clarify the rule, office or decision, not replace it.
4. **Create roles, not mascots:** an SB character or costume should change what a participant can do, not merely brand the event.
5. **Let the material carry the argument:** choose objects whose behaviour—sticking, accumulating, shielding, sounding, recording—does civic work.
6. **Connect spectacle to capability:** every memorable image should point toward knowledge, organisation, recourse or an artifact people keep.
7. **Record risk with the credit:** name who faced the institution and who merely documented, funded or amplified the tactic.
8. **Test hostile reuse:** ask how an image, dataset or playful interaction could be miscaptioned, appropriated, surveilled or used to trivialise the demand.

## Publication cautions

- Use the movement's own name and political demand before describing its aesthetic.
- Avoid “invented by” unless a primary account supports singular authorship.
- Do not claim a policy outcome was caused by an image or performance alone.
- State whether an emotional claim is designed, participant-reported, observed or inferred by SB.
- Link to movement, archive or first-person sources ahead of lifestyle coverage when possible.
- For current or repressive contexts, do not expose identities, operational details or locations beyond what participants have deliberately made public.
- Recheck current legal and political status immediately before publication; the longlist is research, not a live security guide.
