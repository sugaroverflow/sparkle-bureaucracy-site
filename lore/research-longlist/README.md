# Sparkle Bureaucracy Longlist Research

This directory contains the desk-research sprint for finding the projects, organisations, directories, people, and networks that can explain what Sparkle Bureaucracy aims to be.

The research question is:

> What does each candidate teach us about Sparkle Bureaucracy?

The exercise is editorial rather than exhaustive. Interviews and event-based research are deliberately deferred.

## Target output

Four linked, evidence-backed longlists:

- 25 projects.
- 25 organisations.
- 25 project directories or archives.
- 25 relevant people or networks.

The discovery pool may temporarily contain 40–50 projects. The named 25-project longlist is the deduplicated and enriched result, not the raw capture set.

From the project list, select:

- 8–10 projects “we’re inspired by.”
- 4–6 projects doing valuable work where Sparkle is absent or unproven.
- A private reserve list for newsletters, event programming, and later research.

Projects remain the primary research unit. Each project should link to an organisation, relevant people or networks, and the directory or source through which it was discovered.

“Projects we're inspired by” is not synonymous with “projects that satisfy every Sparkle dimension.” A project can belong because it demonstrates one ingredient exceptionally well—such as affective presentation—even when its relationship to bureaucracy or direct civic capacity is indirect. The card should name that partial relationship honestly.

## Research lanes

1. **Bureaucracy made emotionally legible:** games, fiction, installations, performances, and speculative design.
2. **Public-service process redesign:** courts, benefits, immigration, health access, permits, identity, appointments, and casework.
3. **AI inside civic processes:** consultation analysis, case triage, document assembly, synthetic representation, agents, and decision support.
4. **Participatory governance:** voting, deliberation, budgeting, petitions, citizen assemblies, and collective prioritisation.
5. **Organisational rituals:** permission, hiring, evaluation, grants, audits, workplace governance, and invisible labour.
6. **Under-resourced civic capacity:** community-led technology, legal access, migration, mutual aid, local government, and thin-capacity contexts.

## Classifications

### Sparkle exemplar

Strong connection between bureaucracy, affect, technology, and civic capacity.

### Sparkle ancestor

Powerful bureaucratic ritual and affect, but not necessarily AI or civic infrastructure.

### Infrastructure awaiting Sparkle

Useful civic or administrative work with little affective ambition, or no evidence about how it felt.

### Warning case

Technology, playfulness, or friendliness that makes a coercive process more palatable without improving agency or contestability.

These are research relationships, not objective judgments about the quality of the projects.

## Affect and evidence model

Affect has two separate research questions. Do not use the second as a gate for the first.

### Designed affect

What feeling, atmosphere, tension, orientation, or change in perception is built into the work—and how does its form produce it? Evidence can be found in the artifact itself: its interaction, visual language, pacing, ritual, spatial design, copy, mechanics, sound, or deliberate removal of friction. Maker accounts and critical analysis can clarify that reading.

A project can qualify as Sparkly through designed affect without publications, events, surveys, participant quotations, or a formal evaluation. The presentation may itself be the affective intervention.

### Experienced response

What, if anything, do we know about how people actually responded? Distinguish:

- **Participant-reported:** stated by someone who encountered the work.
- **Observed:** reported by a journalist, critic, researcher, or evaluator.
- **Maker-reported:** an audience response described by the maker.
- **Not researched or not found:** no reception evidence was identified in this pass.

Reception evidence can confirm, complicate, or contradict the designed affect. It is required only when the website claims that people actually felt or experienced something—not for recognising affective design.

Evidence confidence:

- **High:** primary project documentation plus strong independent or participant evidence.
- **Medium:** credible primary description with partial independent support.
- **Low:** incomplete, self-promotional, indirect, stale, or ambiguous evidence.

## Editorial tests

### Explanatory value

Does the candidate help a reader understand an ingredient of Sparkle quickly?

### Evidence quality

Can the factual description and emotional claim be supported by appropriate sources?

### Portfolio value

Does it add a process, geography, community, technology, affect, or tension missing from the rest of the selection?

The final portfolio should include pleasant and unsettling affect; fictional and real institutions; AI and non-AI references; deployed and experimental work; community-led and government-led projects; multiple geographies; and both successes and revealing near-misses.

## Website card

Each published project should answer:

> **What is it?**
>
> **What bureaucracy does it touch?**
>
> **What feeling or perception is designed into it—and how?**
>
> **What, if anything, do we know about audience response?**
>
> **What does Sparkle Bureaucracy borrow from it?**
>
> **What remains missing or unresolved?**
