## 1. Project Summary (Living Abstract)

### One-sentence summary

Sparkle Bureaucracy is an experiment lab for prototyping optimistic organisational futures for the age of AI, especially where civic, public-sector, and community systems rely on forms, queues, checkpoints, evaluations, and other bureaucratic rituals.

### Political / civic community

Initial communities:
- Newspeak House fellows, faculty, and adjacent civic technology networks.
- Public-sector AI, civic tech, service design, creative bureaucracy, and participatory democracy practitioners.
- Communities experimenting with AI-native coordination, evaluation, feedback, and institutional legitimacy.

Potential later communities:
- Local government teams and public-service designers.
- Community organisers running decision processes, events, or feedback channels.
- Civic technology projects that need transparent evaluation, community validation, or trust-building rituals.

### Core user need / problem

People are being asked to trust increasingly automated institutional systems, but many of those systems feel opaque, hostile, extractive, or illegible. The project asks whether bureaucratic procedures can be redesigned as participatory, legible, and even delightful without pretending that the underlying power dynamics disappear.

### Core hypothesis

If bureaucratic rituals are redesigned as explicit, playful, inspectable experiments, then civic communities can better understand, contest, and reshape AI-era organisational systems. The strongest outputs may not be "policy advice"; they may be reusable patterns, artifacts, rituals, and evidence that help institutions and communities imagine different ways of working.

### Current version of the concept

The prototype is Sparkle Bureaucracy: an experiment lab. It currently has three connected layers:

1. The lab identity and public narrative: what Sparkle Bureaucracy is, why it matters, and what kind of work belongs inside it.
2. The experiment programme: a set of candidate experiments that can test specific claims about legitimacy, participation, synthetic voice, and bureaucratic experience.
3. The research infrastructure: field notes, feedback logs, experiment cards, and potentially an agent-assisted diary that helps turn scattered notes into publishable evidence.

### 2.2 Risks & Unknowns

| Risk / Unknown | Mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| The experiment lab could feel too broad to assess. | Treat Sparkle Bureaucracy itself as the prototype, but make the lab assessable through a clearly documented first experiment sequence with dates, methods, risks, and evidence. |
| The diary becomes a manifesto rather than evidence of adaptation. | Keep the README narrative, but use this file plus weekly entries, engagement logs, feedback logs, and experiment logs for assessment evidence. |
| Agent-assisted diary output could blur authentic voice and synthetic interpretation. | Treat agents as drafting and triage infrastructure only. Require human approval before publishing. Preserve source links and distinguish notes from generated synthesis. |
| Faculty feedback may be scattered across conversations and comments. | Track questions, feedback, and responses in [faculty-feedback.md](./faculty-feedback.md). |
| Civic use evidence may remain informal. | Log each demo, conversation, event, testimonial, metric, quote, and design change in [engagement-log.md](./engagement-log.md). |
| Playful bureaucracy could trivialise coercive or serious systems. | Add an ethics/risk section to each experiment card. Ask faculty where the line is for each candidate domain. |
| The technical infrastructure could become heavier than the research needs. | Start with markdown and exported Matrix notes. Build automation only when repeated manual work creates real friction. |
| Prior art and novelty claims may be underspecified. | Maintain [prior-art.md](./prior-art.md) and identify how each experiment differs from previous civic tech, creative bureaucracy, and participatory democracy tools. |
